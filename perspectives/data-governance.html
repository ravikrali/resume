Governance should make teams faster, or it has failed

Governance built as a permission queue gets routed around. Governance built as paved roads, defaults and machine-readable policy gets used, because it is the path of least resistance.

Director, AI & Data Management · Enterprise Data Architect Point of view

The requirement this answers

“Establish enterprise data governance frameworks, policies and stewardship”

Encode the policy so the platform enforces it, and reserve human judgement for genuine ambiguity.

The committee trap

Most data governance programmes are org charts with a policy PDF attached. They produce a council, a glossary nobody reads, and a stewardship queue that grows faster than it clears — because the system never learns from what the stewards decide.

The measure of a governance programme is not how many policies exist. It is whether a data engineer shipping something on Tuesday hits a guardrail automatically, or has to remember one.

Policy as code

At KPMG I built an enterprise policy engine that integrated organisational policy, technical metadata, data quality and privacy requirements into controls the platform could actually enforce. Classification drives access. Retention drives lifecycle. Quality thresholds drive whether a build passes. Nobody has to consult a document.

That programme cut compliance remediation cost by around 30%, and the mechanism was straightforward: fewer decisions left to memory.

Stewardship as adjudication

Stewards should not be clearing queues of near-identical tasks. They should be setting policy, resolving genuinely ambiguous cases, and approving rules proposed in plain language — with the system learning from each decision so the same class of case does not return. That is a role elevation, not a headcount reduction, and it needs to be framed that way to the people doing it.

How I apply it

  • Define DQ dimensions, thresholds and KPIs tied to business consequence, not abstract quality
  • Make classification, retention and access machine-enforced at the platform layer
  • Design steward workflows around active learning so queues shrink by construction
  • Publish a decision-rights matrix — who decides, who is consulted, who is informed

What good looks like

  • Engineers can name the guardrails without looking them up
  • Stewardship queue size trends down, not up
  • Audit evidence is generated, not assembled in a panic
  • Governance is invoked in design reviews by delivery teams, unprompted
